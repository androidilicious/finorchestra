Finorchestra: where every number comes from

One real Friday, 2026-07-31, followed from the raw data to the final split. Every number on these slides is the one the system recorded that day, and each slide says how it was computed.

Words with a dotted line, like volatility, have a one-line meaning. Hover, tap, or tab to them.

Arrow keys or the buttons below. "Scroll view" makes one long page.

The job

Every Friday, split one dollar across ten funds

Use only facts that were public that day. Obey the bank's rules. Hold for one week. Repeat.

We did this on 114 Fridays, from 2024-07-05 to 2026-09-04. Every one of those dates comes after the AI model's training ended. So it could not remember what happened next.

Three players make the split each week. Two simple yardsticks sit beside them. At the end we compare what each earned.

why "public that day" matters

Numbers on the economy get revised later. We use the first version, as it stood that Friday. Using today's revised number would be cheating.

why one week

Prices are checked on Friday close. The split is held until the next Friday close. That gives one return per week.

The players

Three ways to decide, two ways to compare

Player 1 · the fitted formula

A regression fitted on past weeks only, refit every Friday. It has no opinion of its own. It is the floor.

Player 2 · AI, clipped

A language model reads the numbers and the Fed's words. It gives views. The solver trims them to fit the rules. The model is not told.

Player 3 · AI, told

Same first views. When a rule binds, a checker writes one sentence back. The model revises, up to twice.

Yardstick A · the neutral mix

A split computed from data each week with no opinion in it. Every rule is measured against it. Holding it is "do nothing".

Yardstick B · 60/40

60% stocks, 40% mid Treasury bonds. The classic simple mix. It ignores the bank's rules.

The point of the test: does a reasoning layer add value over a formula? And does telling it about the rules help?

Step 2 · Z-scores

How unusual is each number? The z-score

z-score = (today's value − its 10-year average) ÷ its 10-year spread

The spread is the standard deviation: the typical distance from the average. A z of 0 is normal. A z of 2 is rare. The average and spread use only the 10 years before today's reading.

Step 4 · Regime

Chance that growth is "up" = share of past weeks that had a lower growth theme.

We keep every past week's theme values. Today's growth theme is +0.08. It is higher than 148 of the 276 past weeks and equal to 2 more, which count half. So the chance that growth is up is about 54%.

Today's inflation theme is +0.16. It is higher than only 41 of 276 past weeks. So the chance that inflation is up is about 15%.

Step 6 · Risk

What we mean by risk: volatility and correlation

Volatility = the spread of a fund's weekly returns × √52.

Take the last 104 weekly returns (2024-08-09 to 2026-07-31). Find their spread. Multiply by √52, about 7.2, to state it per year. The spread here divides by the number of weeks, 104, as the shrinkage method below does. So the two columns match.

FundWeekly spread× √52Yearly volatility
US stocks2.145%→15.5%
long Treasury bonds1.620%→11.7%
inflation-protected bonds0.550%→4.0%
gold2.715%→19.6%
cash (T-bills)0.015%→0.1%

Correlation is how two funds move together. It runs from −1 to +1. Inflation-protected and mid Treasury bonds: +0.90 as measured, +0.71 after the pull-in below. Stocks and long Treasuries: -0.04 as measured, +0.01 after.

shrinkage = 25%

104 weeks is a small sample. So each pair's link is noisy. We pull each one 25% of the way toward the average link, which was +0.15 that window. Each fund keeps its own risk. A known method sets the amount (Ledoit and Wolf, 2004). We do not.

Step 7 · Neutral mix

The neutral mix: weight each fund by one over its volatility

weight = (1 ÷ volatility) ÷ the sum of all the (1 ÷ volatility)

Calm funds get more. Jumpy funds get less. Cash is left out: it is where the solver retreats to, not a position to be neutral in. The sum of the nine "1 ÷ volatility" values is 123.7.

Worked line: inflation-protected bonds: 1 ÷ 3.97% = 25.2. Then 25.2 ÷ 123.7 = 20.4%.

why this and not "market weights"

Version 1 typed in a market mix by hand. Nobody could say where it came from. This one is computed from data each week with no numbers typed in.

Step 9 · Duration

Duration: how hard a bond fund moves when rates move

Duration ≈ percent price move for a one-point move in yields.

We estimate it from data. Take the fund's 104 weekly returns and the weekly change in the 10-year Treasury yield. Fit a line. The slope, with its sign flipped, is the duration.

Worked line: long Treasury bonds: slope -14.3, so duration 14.3 years. A one-point rise in yields cost about 14% that window.

Stocks, gold, commodities and the dollar get 0 by rule. Duration is a bond idea; a fitted number for gold would be noise.

Step 11 · Blend

Turning views into expected returns (Black-Litterman)

Start from the returns that would make the neutral mix the best choice. Pull each fund toward the model's view, by its confidence.

The starting returns are the "prior". Funds that move together move together here too: a view on one bond shifts the others a little.

Then ask: with no rules at all, what split do these returns want? The last column. It wants 511% in inflation-protected bonds, 120% short in long Treasury bonds, and it borrows 449% of the book in cash to pay for it. Nobody can hold that. That is why the next step exists.

One week says nothing. We collect 113 of them.

Sharpe = average weekly return above cash ÷ its spread × √52

AI, told: average 0.0531% a week, spread 0.6225%. 0.0531 ÷ 0.6225 × 7.21 = 0.615, call it 0.62. Around 0.5 is ordinary. 1.0 is very good. Below 0 lost to cash.

p-value: the luck test

Take the two players' weekly returns. Reshuffle them in blocks of 8 weeks, 2,000 times. Count how often the gap flips sign. The gap is in Sharpe. Told minus clipped: +0.27 (0.615 − 0.344), p = 0.41. Below 0.05 would mean "probably not luck". This is not below 0.05.

the feedback loop

A rule bound on the first proposal in 77 of 114 weeks. Revision cleared every rule in 18 of them. Most common: the cap on US dollar (31 weeks), the volatility limit (31).

the honest reading

Neither AI player beat the neutral mix by more than luck allows. Telling the model about the rules helped in direction, not by enough to call. The fitted formula did best on these two years but scored 0.09 over ten years in the mock run. Two years cannot separate skill from a good stretch.

In version 1, with hand-typed weights and fixed 30% caps, "told beats clipped" looked real (p = 0.03). It did not survive the change to data-based rules. That is the biggest lesson of the project so far.

Assumed vs measured

Which numbers are measured, and which are still chosen

measured from data or a public rule
  • Neutral mix: from each fund's risk.
  • Durations: fitted against the 10-year yield.
  • Capital charges: the US bank rule (12 CFR 217).
  • Season odds: each theme's rank in its own past.
  • Pull-in of the links between funds: a known method.
  • Formula weights: fitted again each week.
  • Luck-test spread: measured across the players.
still chosen, and written down
  • The five rule stand-ins: 15 points, 1.25, 2 years, 1.25, 25%.
  • The 10-year window for "normal", and the ±3 cap on z-scores.
  • Which series make each theme.
  • The 104-week window for risk and duration.
  • Risk aversion 3, from past studies.
  • Two revision rounds.

Each of these is written in every run's record, with its basis. The full list is in the assumptions register.

The statistics and market words, in one line each

WordMeaning

yield curveThe 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year. Steep means easy money ahead; flat or negative, tight.
credit spreadExtra yield risky companies pay over the government. High means fear.

breakevenThe inflation rate bond prices imply over 10 years. The market's inflation expectation.
